# US Entity Structure Guide

A working comparison of the realistic legal homes for a mission-driven technology
organisation in the United States: a **501(c)(3) public charity**, a **501(c)(6)
business/trade league**, and an ordinary **for-profit company** (LLC, C-corp, or
benefit corporation) — plus what changes depending on **which state** you
incorporate in.

Written for founders who need to make one decision once, and who will be living
with the filings, the tax posture and the governance constraints for years.

> **Not legal or tax advice.** This is a research summary to make you a better
> client of a lawyer, not a substitute for one. Rules change; verify anything
> load-bearing against the primary sources in [docs/sources.md](docs/sources.md)
> before you file.

## The one-paragraph version

If your work is **research, education, or public benefit** and you want
tax-deductible donations and foundation grants, you want a **501(c)(3)** — and
you accept an absolute ban on political campaign activity, a cap on lobbying, a
public Form 990, and the fact that nobody owns the assets. If your work serves a
**common business interest of an industry or membership** — standards, advocacy,
convening — a **501(c)(6)** buys you unlimited lobbying and looser purpose
constraints at the price of non-deductible dues and no foundation grants. If you
need **equity, speed, and the ability to keep the upside**, form a **for-profit**;
you can still be mission-locked through a benefit corporation or contract.
Most serious organisations end up with **two entities**, not one.

## Scope and caveats

- Federal tax law is national; state law is not. Every state number here is
  point-in-time and needs re-checking at filing.
- "Where to incorporate" and "where to register to do business" are different
  questions, and the second one is the expensive one. See
  [docs/states.md](docs/states.md).
- Nothing here covers 501(c)(4) social welfare orgs in depth, private
  foundations, or non-US structures.
