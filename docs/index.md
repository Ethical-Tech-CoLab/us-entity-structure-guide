A working comparison of the realistic legal homes for a mission-driven technology
organisation in the United States: a **501(c)(3) public charity**, a **501(c)(6)
business or trade league**, and an ordinary **for-profit company** (LLC, C-corp,
or benefit corporation) — plus what changes depending on **which state** you
incorporate in.

Written for founders who need to make one decision once, and who will be living
with the filings, the tax posture and the governance constraints for years.

> **Not legal or tax advice.** This is a research summary meant to make you a
> better client of a lawyer, not a substitute for one. Rules change; verify
> anything load-bearing against the primary sources before you file.

## The one-paragraph version

If your work is **research, education, or public benefit** and you want
tax-deductible donations and foundation grants, you want a **501(c)(3)** — and
you accept an absolute ban on political campaign activity, a cap on lobbying, a
public Form 990, and the fact that nobody owns the assets. If your work serves a
**common business interest of an industry or membership** — standards, advocacy,
convening — a **501(c)(6)** buys you unlimited lobbying and looser purpose
constraints at the price of non-deductible dues and no foundation grants. If you
need **equity, speed, and the ability to keep the upside**, form a
**for-profit**; you can still be mission-locked through a benefit corporation or
by contract. Most serious organisations end up with **two entities**, not one.

## In three lines

- **c3** buys you money you cannot otherwise raise, at the price of control and
  political voice.
- **c6** buys you political voice and purpose flexibility, at the price of the
  donor deduction and the grant pipeline.
- **For-profit** buys you speed, capital and ownership, at the price of tax and
  of every funder and customer who can only work with a charity.
